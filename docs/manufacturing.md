# Manufacturing

A part has to be made. FielDes has three blocks for what a process asks of it, in the **Engineering** menu under **Manufacturing**:

| Block | What it is | |
|---|---|---|
| `manufacturing_constraint`, `fff_constraint`, `cnc_constraint`, `molding_constraint` | **constraints**: what the part has to keep | inputs, like a support or a load |
| `manufacturing_analysis(part, constraints=[...])` | the **check**: where the part breaks them, as colours on it, and how much | a result, like a stress |
| `manufacturing_optimization(part, constraints=[...])` | the **shell**: the part, **changed** until it keeps them, and what was changed | a body: wherever a body goes, it goes |

```python
printer = fff_constraint(nozzle=0.4)
report = manufacturing_analysis(part, constraints=[printer])        # coloured where it breaks a rule
better = manufacturing_optimization(part, constraints=[printer])    # the part changed, coloured by the geometry change
```

## Constraints and their rules

`manufacturing_constraint` has every rule, each optional -- only what is written is asked. The presets write the numbers of a process; every number
of a preset is an argument of it, and the guides they come from are not a standard (ask your printer's and your plastic's makers).

| Rule | Asks | Measured |
|---|---|---|
| `overhang=45` | the steepest a surface that faces down may lean, in degrees from the vertical, without a support under it | the angle of the surface to the vertical; the first layer on the plate rests on it |
| `min_wall=0.8` | the thinnest wall, mm | the thickness along the normal, through the material (not where the surface turns within half the limit -- the rounded edge of a thin wall is not a thin wall) |
| `min_gap=0.4` | the narrowest gap between walls, a hole or a slot, mm | the width along the normal, through the air |
| `max_bridge=20` | the longest flat span with nothing under it, mm | the narrowest chord of the air under a flat roof that has a wall at both ends (a ledge with an open end is an overhang, not a bridge) |
| `min_radius=3` | the smallest radius of an inside corner, mm | whether a ball of that radius reaches into it (a corner is marked from about 17 % short of the radius: see The check) |
| `draft=1.0` | the least slope of a wall away from `pull_direction`, degrees | the angle of the wall to the pull direction |
| `undercut_along=(0, 0, 1)` | nothing a tool or a mould half coming along this direction, from either side, cannot reach | a ray along it, both ways, meets the part |

`build_direction` is the way up of a print (default z); `None` leaves it to be found: of the six axis directions and 40 spread over the sphere, the one that leaves the least of the surface leaning out
(with an overhang rule; the first 0.6 mm, resting on the plate, is not leaning out), and of those the one that makes the lowest print. It is a start, not a search for the best way to stand a part: say it when you know it. The plate is taken at the lowest corner of the part's box along the way up, which is the part's own lowest point for an axis and lies below it for a way up that is not an axis -- a face laid on the plate at an angle is then not taken to rest on it and counts as leaning out, so the search prefers the axes; to print a tilted part on its flat face, turn the part until the face is level and name that axis. `pull_direction` is the way a mould opens. `region=` is a shape: the constraint holds only inside it, so a part can be given a constraint for each of its regions --
the **overhang** rule is off in the presets (slicers put supports where they are needed) and is for what supports cannot reach: a channel from a flow or a thermal topology
optimisation, a bore, a cosmetic face, a lattice. `overhang_fix='fill'` (the default) grows the body under the overhang; `'cut'` cuts the air above it into a **teardrop**.
`gap_fix='fill'` closes a gap that is too narrow, `'widen'` makes it as wide as asked.

| Preset | Writes | From |
|---|---|---|
| `fff_constraint(nozzle=0.4)` | `min_wall` two nozzle widths, `min_gap` one, `max_bridge` 20 mm | guides give 0.8 to 1.2 mm of wall for a 0.4 mm nozzle, a gap narrower than the nozzle prints closed, bridges up to about 30 mm; the 45 degrees of overhang depends on the material and the printer: calibrate |
| `cnc_constraint(tool_diameter=6)` | `min_radius` the cutter's radius, `min_gap` its diameter, `undercut_along` the axis, `min_wall` 0.8 mm | the smallest radius a cutter leaves is its own; thin walls chatter (1.5 mm and more for plastics) |
| `molding_constraint(min_wall=1)` | `draft` 1 degree, `undercut_along` the pull, `min_radius` half the wall | 1 degree is the baseline, 2 for a texture; 0.5 times the wall inside; walls 1 to 4 mm by the plastic |

A rule counts as broken when it is more than 5 % short of its limit: what the part's field can say of a size is not exact.

## The check

`manufacturing_analysis` reads the part's own field and nothing else: **every rule is a field on the part**, drawn as colours on it (the result card switches rule; `result.min_wall`
is the field), and nothing is meshed. The normals are read from the field's symbolic derivative (`Shape.partial`: the kernel derives the field's expression; each component is the mean of the derivative at two points a thousandth of the part's size apart, which is what a central difference is, with the derivative's own bounds); a thickness is found along the normal, by where the field crosses zero
between fourteen places; a ball that does not fit into a corner is found by the field at probes along the normal and on the ball's sphere: how far from the part the centre of a ball that touches the point can be, read at the centres along the normal, on the ball's sphere and tilted 12 degrees round the normal and -- for a point that still reads short -- on cones of 24 to 70 degrees round it (on the surface of an optimizer a point is often on a crease between two facets, where the normal is the mean of the two and the centre of the ball that touches it is up to 70 degrees from it; a corner that is too tight has no centre that fits at any of them) and, for a point that still reads short, by looking round the best centre found -- eight ways at a smaller angle each round, six rounds -- because the centre of a ball that touches a point at a free end of a corner, or on the bowl over a hole, is 30 to 60 degrees from the normal and has to be located to a few tenths of a millimetre: whether a ball of the radius touches the point is where the field is largest on that sphere. The sphere is looked at as a whole first, in 120 directions, and the search round the best centres begins from the three best of them (33 of the 38 samples that were marked on an L whose corner is rounded to a radius of 6 had a ball that fits, 5.9 to 6.0 mm clear against the 5.7 asked, measured by 1500 rays from its centre to the surface, and the search had started on the wrong side of the sphere); and the clearance is read as the field says it, not divided by a slope read at one place -- the field of the context has been brought to a slope of 1 as a whole, and the slope at the centre along the normal, 1.1 to 1.3 on that L, is not where the best centre lies: dividing by it made a ball that fits read 20 % short. The L passes the independent check at 0.05 %, where it was marked on 0.58 %. In a corner of 90 degrees that
clearance grows by 0.29 mm for every mm of the corner's radius, so a corner is marked from about 17 % short of the limit (under 2.5 mm for a limit of 3); between that and the limit (2.5 to 2.85 mm) the reading is
within its own noise -- the normal is the field's slope averaged over a step, and a normal 5 degrees off at a radius of 3 moves the ball by 0.26 mm -- and a corner there may or may not be marked, in stripes along the arc where it is (seen on pockets with corners of 1.8 and 2.7 mm). A normal that is a degree or two off -- the normal of a field made
from a mesh -- does not mark a corner that is right (at the radius asked or more).

**How the colours are read.** A rule that looks at a normal does not judge the places near an edge (the normal there is a mix of the two faces, not the lean of either), so on a part made of boxes with
sharp edges every corner of the mesh that is drawn lies on an edge and reads "kept" -- while the underside of a box that hangs out is one big flat face that breaks the rule all over. The colour of a check is
therefore not left to the corners: its triangles are looked at inside, as those of the change colour of `manufacturing_optimization` are (a triangle longer than eight minimum features is cut into four, again
and again, and one whose corners differ is cut down to a four-thousandth of the part), and the check is read at the new corners. The underside of the T of the guided tour is red, with the edge of the red where the
check puts it, and the report's 12 % is the share of the surface that the picture shows. It costs one more read of the check at those corners: 0.37 s, and 0.39 s at the finer level, on the T of the guided tour (the console says how long it took, with the number of vertices before and after).

**The foot of a wall in the picture.** What the window draws of a result is a lattice copy of it meshed at the resolution of the view, and the corner at the foot of a wall that lies exactly on a plane of the mesher's grid -- a part on integer coordinates, which is most of them -- used to be drawn with a small wing (0.75 mm sideways and 0.5 mm below the floor at resolution 3; the numbers, the checks and the exports never had it). The cause was in the mesher, not in the field: at such a corner the field gave a derivative of 1e-8, float noise of a branch with no slope, and normalised it pointed anywhere, with a plane 8000 mm from the sample, which pulled the vertex of the cell one cell width out. A sample whose plane is farther from it than two cells is left out of the vertex now. `FIELDES_DC_TRACE=x,y,z,r` prints what the cells of the mesher within r of a point were given, for whoever meets another such place.

The **numbers** of the report -- how much of the surface breaks a rule, how badly, in which places -- are **measured**, on a few thousand points of the surface (`samples=`, 5000):
random points near it, each moved onto it by Newton steps along the gradient. They are worked out a second way -- plain arithmetic on the values of the field at points along the lines
each rule looks along -- which is a check of the colours: the fields and the numbers are made two ways. The points are never drawn and never used to build anything.

**The check does not share with the shell what finds a fault, so that it can verify it.** The code is in four files: `manufacturing_rules.py` (the constraints and the presets: what is asked;
nothing is measured), `manufacturing_field.py` (how a part's field is read: moved lookups, normals, values at points; no rule), `manufacturing_check.py` (`manufacturing_analysis`) and
`manufacturing.py` (`manufacturing_optimization` and its remedies). The check imports nothing of the optimization; the optimization calls one function of the check, `_analyse` (private), to be told what its
result still breaks. So `manufacturing_analysis` on a result is a verification of the remedies and not their own reading of themselves. Where the report measures a wall or a gap it has a way of its own:
the shortest of nine lines through the material (the air, for a gap) from the surface point -- the normal and eight leaning 20 degrees away from it -- where the remedies find a thin wall by the line
along the normal alone; what one of them misses the other may find.

```text
Manufacturing analysis: 5000 surface samples (10.8 s)
  Rule violations:
    Wall thickness (limit 0.8 mm): satisfied
    Gap width (limit 6 mm): 0.56 % of surface area, max. deviation 4.02 mm, 3 region(s)
        (7.904, 11.92, 5.139)  0.46 %
        (32, 21.83, 10.98)  0.08 %
        (5.65, 11.91, 13.97)  0.02 %
  Evaluated on the part's own field; not a substitute for the manufacturer's own check.
```

What the check reads, and how far: a wall that is thinner than the spacing of the probes, far off, is missed by the undercut check; a thickness is along the normal (a sloping thin wall reads right, a thin web
seen end-on does not); the part's field should be about a distance (a part imported from STEP has one that is a lower bound of it, so its walls read thinner than they are; so has a part made by taking a union of overlapping cutters out of a body, inside the cavity -- there the field is the deepest of the cutters' own depths, 1.4 mm where the wall is 3 mm away -- and the rules that read values up to a ball's radius from the surface, the corner rule above all, take a point in the middle of such a wall for a corner: run `exact_distance` on that part first).
**It is a check of the part's own field and not a substitute for the check of the one who makes it.**

## The shell

`manufacturing_optimization` changes the **geometry**. Each remedy is a field made from the part's own -- shifted copies of it -- in the region of its constraint:

| Rule | Remedy |
|---|---|
| `min_wall` | the surface is offset outwards by what the wall lacks, half on each side, to the limit and a margin of 4 % so that the check passes it; a wall within the 5 % grace of the limit is left as it is. How thin the wall is is the length of the line along the normal, from the nearest point of the surface through the material to where it meets the surface again -- the thickness the check measures --, and the offset acts only where that line starts on a face and ends on a face that faces back at it (a wall is two faces facing each other: a flat face that a sheet meets at a glancing angle, the cut face of a lattice, has a wedge behind it, which is not a wall). The whole face is thickened, up to its edges and not past them (measured: a 0.6 fin asked for 0.8 comes out 0.800 over its whole face and stands out 0.025 mm past its top), and where the growth ends on a face that turns smoothly the end of it is a smooth ramp, not a step. **It is worked out once, by the kernel, on a lattice laid over the part (a 400th of its size)** wherever the lattice can hold the wall (3.4 nodes or more; a finer wall is the formula): the growth is spread and smoothed along the surface over a fifth of the limit -- never round the end of a plate or the corner of a block into the air beside it --, which is what keeps the rim of a lattice cut by a block clean and not ragged (a gyroid of 1.0 mm walls asked for 1.6: 1.60 to 1.64 mm along the normal at the ten places measured, in 3.4 s, and it draws as fast as the lattice does: 3 s where the formula took 46 s). **What a thickened face cannot do:** where a flat face cuts a sheet at a shallow angle, the cut leaves a knife edge, a wedge whose tip has no thickness whatever is done to the sheet, because a thickened face only moves the tip along the flat face. The check marks such a wedge within the wall limit of the flat face (6.5 % of the surface that lies there, for a gyroid cut by a block) and finds every wall farther from a cut face at the rule. Along an edge where thin walls meet and in the inside corner where a thin wall stands on a thick face the growth stops at the corner as the faces do; ask `min_radius` as well to have the corner a radius of your own. The wall is measured along the normal, which is the thickness where the two faces are parallel; where both faces curve towards each other with a radius about the wall's own thickness (a web between two small holes) the closest approach of the faces is shorter than the chord along the normal, and the check, which reads nine lines through the wall, finds the web up to about 10 % under the limit there (a web of 0.3 between holes of radius 1, asked 0.8: 0.800 along the line of centres, 0.71 to 0.73 on the oblique lines); the report lists those places |
| `min_gap` | the gap is closed, or made as wide as asked: each side grows by half the gap's width (the length of the line along the normal through the air, as the check measures it), up to the gap's mouth and not above it. A point of the air that is nearer the face at the bottom of a slot than either of its sides has a line along its normal that goes up out of the gap, so it is asked again from the point half the limit above that face along its normal, where a side is the nearest face: where the answer there is "a gap", the point is part of the result, and no sheet of air is left closed in over the floor of a slot (0.34 mm of one 0.7 wide under a rule of 1.0 before); over a groove shallower than half the limit the question is asked out in the open and nothing is added, a V that is open at the top. The field is read through differences of a step of a thousandth of the part's size (0.03 mm for a part of 30 mm, never under 0.004 mm): a slit narrower than two of those steps -- the point of a V-shaped slot, the first 0.06 mm above the floor of a slit -- is below that resolution and stays as it was (draw and judge such a small part in a scene of its own) |
| `min_radius` | a **ball of that radius rolled in the air along the part**. An inside corner is filled by the arc of the ball that rolls in it, along the whole of it (a circle of that radius whose slope turns smoothly from one face to the other); a slot, a gap or a hole narrower than the ball is filled up to the arc of the ball resting on its edges (the ball's sag: a slot 4 mm wide under a ball of radius 3 stays 0.76 mm below its mouth, and the hole of 5 mm in a plate is filled in its middle with the two bowls of the ball); an outside corner, a bump and a flat face are as they were. It is the kernel's rolling ball (`libfive_field_overhang_lattice`, mode 5; `_corner_maxfilter`), the same for every part -- a small block, an import, a lattice, the topology-optimized bracket -- and nothing is walked at the points the mesher samples: the part's field is read once on a lattice (a 400th of the part's size, coarser for a part that would need more than thirty million nodes) and the kernel makes of it the **true distance** to the surface (the field of a union of boxes is more than a distance beyond an edge, and a ball rolled on that was a fifth short in volume: the sources of a distance transform are the points where the field changes sign along the edges of the lattice, which lie on the surface also along the sharp rim of a hole, and the distance of a node is the least over the sources round its nearest one), finds on the ball round every node of the air the largest value of that distance (at 96 directions, refined round the best of them to a few hundredths of a millimetre), fills the node where no ball that lies in the air covers it (with a tolerance of a small part of a node, which keeps a flat face and a convex edge exactly as they are), and hands back the exact distance to the filled part, so that what reads the clearance of a ball's centre from the result -- the check does -- reads a distance and not the margin of the ball test. The result is a field of its own, a few seconds to make and next to nothing to draw. Measured: the fillet of an L of radius 6 adds 144.9 mm^3 where the arc says 154.5 (the last millimetre of the corner at each free end is a ball swinging out past the end, 4 %, and the tails of the arc are a few hundredths of a millimetre short of their tangent points; the arc itself is within 0.01 mm of the exact one on its diagonal); the independent check, which looks for a ball of the radius at every sample, marks 1.04 % of the CNC sampler's surface (52 of 5000 samples, at none of which a ball fits, found by 6000 directions on the sphere): all of them at the free ends of the two gap fills, where the surface of a fill ends in a ramp, the median 0.05 mm short of the radius asked and the worst 0.35 mm, an eighth of the radius, two nodes of the lattice; the narrow slot above is solid up to the arc of the ball at three places across it and all the way down; the floor corners of a slot 8 mm wide are arcs of the ball to 0.2 mm at every 10 degrees; a round boss's ring of fillet adds 31 mm^3 (34.8 by hand); a pocket whose corners are rounded to 1.5 mm is made 3 mm and one rounded to 5 mm is not touched. The remedy is made only where the check of the part finds a corner short of the radius: a part that keeps the rule comes out as it was, and the card says "no change required". The library's `fillet` (the same ball walked in the part's own field, see [fields](fields.md)) is what a small part falls back on when no lattice can be made. **A gap rule that the corner radius already covers is not made twice**: a ball of radius r cannot enter a gap narrower than 2 r (a cutter's radius is half its diameter, which is its gap rule), so when a constraint asks a radius of at least half the gap, less the grace, the rounding closes the gap up to the arc and the gap step is left out; the card says so |
| `draft` | what it is for: a moulded part leaves the mould in one direction, and a wall parallel to it rubs along the steel for its whole height (it sticks, scuffs, needs ejector force), so moulding guides ask 1 to 2 degrees of lean on every such wall. What it does: a wall that leans less than the draft is offset by what its slope lacks, in proportion to how far it is below the top of the part: a vertical wall comes out wider at the foot, by that depth times the tangent of the draft (0.35 mm at 10 mm for 2 degrees). Tops and floors are not moved; every sideways wall of the part gets it, the walls of holes included. **The foot of a wall that grew is closed**: a point just above the floor the wall stands on, between the floor and the plane at 45 degrees through the corner, is nearer to the floor than to the wall, the floor does not grow, and a wedge of air as deep as the growth (0.4 mm at 2 degrees on a wall 12 high, about 1 mm when the walls are thickened too) would stay under the grown wall -- an undercut along the pull at the foot of every wall. Such a point, whose place one growth farther along the pull (away from the floor; the same under a roof) lies in the grown skin of a wall, is made part of the result too, so the lean of the wall goes on down to the floor, with no vertical stripe in place of it (measured on the moulding sampler: the independent check marks the draft on 0.02 % of the surface and the undercut on 0.02 %, one sample of 5000 each). On the middle plane of a slot narrower than the draft makes it, where the slopes of its two faces cancel and a central difference reads no normal, the normals are read by forward and by backward differences and the larger growth is taken (a seam of air 0.15 mm wide down the middle of a slot of 0.3 mm), and when the walls are thickened as well the growths of both are added in one offset whose band is 1.2 times their sum and two steps wide |
| `undercut_along` | what lies both under and over part of the part, along the direction, is filled: it is worked out once, by the kernel, on a lattice laid along the direction (a pitch of a 250th of the part's size, coarser for a part that would need more than twelve million nodes): the part is sampled at the nodes and in every column the air between the first and the last node of the part is filled, so that the edge of what is filled is one straight wall under the tip of a lip and not a staircase, and a shelf is seen down to a 250th of the part's length along the direction. The formula (the part's field read at 17 fixed heights, a shelf down to a sixteenth) is what a lattice that cannot be made falls back on |
| `overhang` | `'fill'`: a point of the air is part of the part when a disc, as wide as the slope has run by the time it is up to the part, fits inside the part at the height where the part starts above it, or at any height up to `max_support_height` higher -- a gusset under a ledge at the angle asked, as deep as the ledge is wide and no deeper. **It is worked out once, by the kernel, on a lattice laid over the part in the build direction (a pitch of a 250th of the part's size, coarser for a part that would need more than twelve million nodes) and kept as a field of its own, which the result unites with the part**: the part is sampled at the nodes, the distance in each slice from the part to the air is found to the edge itself, and in each column the lowest height the gusset reaches. A point of the drawing then costs one look at the part and one at that field, where the same rule asked as a formula at every point cost about a hundred looks at the part. The faces of the gusset are exact planes; a crease between two of them is rounded within one pitch; **the flank leans 5 % under the limit** (43.5 degrees for 45), so that it keeps the rule at its creases -- a teardrop's, a cone that meets a cylinder, has none of its surface marked by the independent check at that, and 0.30 % of it marked at 2 % under -- and the fill is made under every face that leans more than the flank does: a plate at 44 degrees, which keeps the rule (it holds up to the limit and 5 % over it, 47.25), is given the sliver between its face and the flank (0.8 mm^3 for a plate 8 x 12 mm), one at 43 degrees nothing; `'cut'`: the same done to the air the other way up -- a teardrop above a channel (measured: the air above a hole of radius 5 follows the ideal 45 degree cap to within 0.1 mm). Nothing is grown or cut under the print plate, and the part's own bottom resting on it is not an overhang. A point is filled when the whole cone at the angle asked above it lies in the part as it was, so under a thin plate that leans only a few degrees over the limit (a plate 2 mm thick, 47 degrees) the cone fits at the foot alone and the rest of the face stays as it was -- the shell lists those places as still violated, and does not claim a clean result; a thicker part, or a wider ledge, is filled whole. Where a gusset ends on the side face of the part a strip of a tenth of a millimetre or two is left, which the check marks (0.04 % of the surface of a ledge) |

They are all made from the part **as it is** (shifted copies of shifted copies would multiply the cost of a field at every step), united -- what they add, then what they cut -- and
what they changed is **measured** (on evenly spread points -- a Halton sequence -- of the shell round the surface for the remedies that change a thin layer -- walls, gaps, corners, draft --, so that a layer a tenth of a millimetre thick has points in it, and on points over the whole box for the others: the volumes are measured, not worked out. Measured on an L of two walls with a fillet of radius 6, 154.5 mm^3 by hand, over 8 starts of the sequence: 153.5 mm^3 on average, 1.0 % between one measurement and the next, 2.2 % off at worst -- on as many random points it was 4.7 % and 8.3 %; the part's own volume 0.1 %, on random points 1.5 %): a remedy that would change more of the part than `max_change` (0.25) allows is **not made**, and says so (a
wide overhang is filled down to a support, which this is not). A gusset is made only where it can be printed: it has to rest on the part or on the plate under it (the wedge in the corner under a ledge that sticks out of a wall does; a keel under a flat underside with nothing below it would start its lowest layer in the air, so it is not made, and those faces stay as they are and are listed as still breaking the rule). A region that is excluded (`exclude`) or in `keep=` is never touched, no gusset is made under it (a lug or a plate that you keep as it is stays as it is, supported or not), and it is not judged either: the shell's own check leaves it out of its numbers (the shares are of the surface outside it), and `manufacturing_analysis(part, constraints, keep=...)` does the same for a check you make yourself. Give the result to the shell again for a second pass.

The result is a **body** that is the part changed, shown as the **geometry change**: **ONE body, the surface of the result, coloured by the change at each point of it** -- red where it lies outside the input part (material added), blue where it lies inside it (material cut), the part's colour where it is as it was. The colour is a class and not an amount: it is the difference of the part's field and the result's at the point, a step from 0 to 1 over a few hundredths of a millimetre (`.change_tolerance`), so a vertex of the drawn mesh that lies a half cell off the surface, at the rim of a hole or the corner of a block, still reads what the face under it reads. The viewer cuts the triangles of the picture where the colour changes -- and the inside of a big face is looked at -- so that the edge of the red is as fine as the smallest triangles, and a face that is red all over, with every corner on its rim, is red. (The result used to be drawn as three bodies -- the part, the material added and the material cut, each by its exact field. The red body of a fillet or a gusset is a sliver that thins to nothing along its edges, which a mesh cannot hold under one cell: saw teeth along both edges of the fillet round a boss; and a second pass over a result painted the first pass's material grey.) A result given to the shell again keeps the red and blue of the first pass; the card says so, and its numbers are those of the last pass. The card has a row for each remedy that was kept ("Draft applied to walls") so that it says what the added material is for. It has `.changes` (each remedy: what it adds or cuts on its own), `.added` (the volume of the union of what the additive remedies add: a place that two of them work on is counted once), `.removed`, `.share`, `.fail_states` (only when asked, `verify=True`: what the shell's own check, made on what it made, still finds broken and
where: the five biggest places of each rule; `.fail_totals` has all the places and their share of the surface for each rule, which is what the card counts) and `.before`, `.after` (the two checks). `.oriented()` is the part turned so that the way up of the print is z (draw it to see the part standing the way it is printed; when the way up was left free, `build_direction=None`, the card has a row "Way up of the print" with the direction the tool found), and an export (`export_mesh(result, "part.3mf")`) writes it **turned**
-- the model in the tree stays as it is. **Run `manufacturing_analysis` on the result**: the shell's check is its own. A call that was made before -- the same part, constraints and numbers, as when a model is shown or hidden and the script runs again -- gives back what it made, in the same session, and does not measure and shape the part again.

What it costs: the fields of the remedies are made of tens to hundreds of shifted copies of the part's field. A field made of fields made of fields used to cost a multiple of itself at every level (a kernel walk that
went down a shared node once for every way to reach it); it is linear now, and a ledge with a gusset is made and checked again in about 1.5 seconds. What a remedy costs to draw is not its number of lookups: the mesher can only skip a cell where the bounds of the field over it decide what is in it, and a formula that finds a thin wall by a walk along the normal has bounds that are never 0 near a face (the foot of the line moves with the cell), so every cell of the band round the whole surface was worked through to the finest level -- a thin fin and a narrow slot in a plate of 70 mm made it draw for more than ten minutes. Two things are done about it. The growth of the remedies is cleared of the kernel's "may be NaN" flag where it joins the part (`nanfill`: a NaN is no growth): a cell whose bounds carry the flag is ambiguous whatever they say, and the mesher keeps its tape unshortened, so nothing below it is pruned -- and the line along the normal is found by a division that is 0 / 0 for an interval across a face, so the flag was set in most cells near one. And the tool puts boxes round the places where the remedies that move the surface (the walls, a gap filled, the draft) do anything at all -- found on the points it measures them on, and from the check of the part -- and says to the mesher that the growth is 0 outside them: the result is the same field (compared at 150,000 points near the surface: no point inside in one and outside in the other), and what the mesher works through is the neighbourhood of the fin and the slot. The plate draws in 90 seconds at 4 cells to the millimetre in the window. The remedies are kept to the least that is exact where it matters (the wall remedy is the offset where a wall is thin and nothing else), and the scene of a test is small and fine rather than large and coarse:
**the finer the picture, the more memory the mesher needs for any of them** (cubed: twice as fine is eight times), and a part with many features costs the copies times its own size. A picture too fine for the machine is the one thing that can stop the viewer; draw at the
resolution the thinnest wall needs (4 cells across it is enough to see it) and no finer. Measured times are under Speed below. A big imported part costs the copies times its own size. The shell does **not** check its own result unless it is asked to (`verify=True`): that check is the whole of the check run again on the result, and on a
topology-optimized bracket it was ten of the thirteen minutes. Run `manufacturing_analysis` on the result when you want it; the card then shows no "places still breaking a rule" until you do. The corner remedy rolls a ball of the radius along the part on a lattice of the kernel (see the table above): nothing is meshed for it.

**A pass of the tool is stable.** The result of the tool, handed to the tool again with the same rules, comes out unchanged: `.added` and `.removed` are 0 and the card says "no change required". Two things make it so. The overhang fill is worked out to its fixed point in one pass: a gusset made once lies beside the part in a slice -- the wall and the gusset on it, merged -- and the discs that fit in the two together are larger than those that fit in either, so a second look would put more under it; the kernel repeats the construction, counting the gusset as part, until nothing is added (the depth a gusset may reach is still measured from the part as it was given). And the remedies that move the surface along its normal -- the walls thickened, the draft, a gap filled -- are added to each other as growths of one offset, so that the face one of them makes (the fin that was thickened) is moved by the others (given its draft); united, each had been made from the part as it was and the faces of one were never seen by the others. The overhang rule counts a face as broken when it leans more than the limit and the grace of every other rule, 5 % of it (47.25 degrees for 45): the field of a result made on a lattice is a lattice node off where a face meets a round one.

**A part that is expensive to read, or whose field is not a distance, is worked out once, on a lattice.** Where a point of the part's field costs more than 15 microseconds (the topology-optimized bracket: 26), or the slope of the field wanders over its surface by more than 0.02 (a lattice's, an optimizer's: a box has none), the remedies come from the kernel as fields of their own instead of formulas that ask the part dozens of times at every point the mesher samples: the **overhang** (a gusset or a teardrop), the **undercut** fill (in every column of a lattice laid along the cutter's axis, the air between the first and the last node of the part, and in the columns next to it as well, so that the wall of the fill stands a hair outside the edge of the cavity and no slit of air is left between a floor and a ceiling; it is made once more, last, from the result when a remedy after it added material -- the fillets of the corners fill a crevice, and the web can put a lid over a column again), the **draft** (the formula of the rule, worked out on the lattice from the part's values in a band round its surface), the **walls** (a wall thinner than the limit is offset outwards by half of what it lacks; the thickness is the length of the line along the normal through the material, read off the lattice) and the **corners** (the rolling ball itself, with no walk; for every part, as the table above says). The lattice is a 400th of the part's size (a 250th for the overhang and the undercut; coarser for a part that would need tens of millions of nodes); a distance read between nodes is good to a small part of a node, so these are placed within a node of the exact formula. A part of exact distance fields (boxes, cylinders, planes and arcs) keeps the formulas for its walls, its draft and its gaps, which are exact at any resolution and at the ends of a small plate, and the undercut fill is a lattice only for a part that is expensive to read; the corners are a lattice for every part. The gap remedy stays a formula. The walls lattice is made at a finer pitch, as far as forty-five million nodes go, where the limit is smaller than three and a half nodes of its pitch: a formula in the middle of a chain of lattices would make everything after it dearer to read. The corners are rounded to the radius and 8 % over it, and the draft is made 8 % over what was asked, for such a part (what the check reads of it is good to a few per cent); a part with an exact field gets the radius as asked and the draft 3 % over (the walls are grown 4 % over the limit for every part). `FIELDES_UNDERCUT_LATTICE`, `FIELDES_DRAFT_LATTICE` and `FIELDES_WALLS_LATTICE` set to 1 force the lattice, to 0 forbid it. Measured on the bracket's mould job: the tool 91 s and the drawing 12 s (it never finished before); see the report.

**Undercut, draft and the radius of the inside corners are one operator on such a part.** When a constraint asks for an undercut rule and a corner radius together (a milling cutter, a mould), the part that is expensive to read gets ONE field from the kernel instead of the two remedies one after the other, because the two undid each other: the walls of an undercut fill are sharp inside corners that the rounding must then round, and the webs the rounding puts into a crevice cap columns of air that the fill had left open -- made by hand in rounds on the optimized bracket, the share of its surface that broke the undercut rule went 0, 1.5, 1.3, 2.7, 3.0 per cent and the inside radius 14, 6, 10, 5, 9 per cent, and each round of the rounding added another 1,400 to 2,200 mm³. The operator fills the air that a ball-end tool of the cutter's radius cannot touch coming along the axis, from either end (a mould half: either half; a mill: from either side of the stock). A ball stands where the part is at least its radius away and the tool can go on out from there (the shaft can be asked to widen by the tangent of a draft for every unit it goes out, and the operator does it -- a wall with a taper of 2 degrees, checked by hand below -- but the tool does not ask it: a lean of one degree over a wall of 10 mm is a skin of 0.19 mm, under half a node of the lattice, and the noise of the distances is the lean, so the draft of a mould is the draft remedy's, made after the operator, and the fill's vertical walls get theirs too; on a lattice a wall that has a roof over it and nothing under it leans the other way, wider at the top -- the half of the mould that forms it is the one pulled down -- where the usual skin, wider at the foot, shut a pocket of air under the roof), and the air that no ball of that kind touches is filled. What is left is the union of the balls that fit, so every point of the result's surface has a ball resting on it and a line along the axis that meets no material, and giving the result to the tool again changes (nearly) nothing; the surface of the fill is the envelope of the balls: the arc of a rolling ball in a corner, a wall that leans where the shaft widens, a vertical wall at the edge of a cavity that nothing reaches. Checked by hand on a corner of two walls (the fillet of a ball of 3 mm: 1.93 mm² a unit of height, the operator 1.97), a slot under a plate (2,080 mm³ by hand, 2,077) and a wall with a taper of 2 degrees (1,676 mm³, 1,745).

## Supports and the print file

A support is a **separate body** of the result, and it is the last resort: what the shell can do by changing the part it does first. `support='block'` on a constraint (with the overhang rule) lets the shell add, under every
surface of its result that still leans out more than the angle asked -- a gusset that was too big for `max_change`, a flat roof -- a **block straight under it**, down to the plate or the part below, kept `support_gap` (0.3 mm) from the part so that it breaks off.
`no_support=` (a shape, or a list) is where none may be made (what leans out over it is not held, and the card lists it). The result then has `.supports` (a field, drawn beside the part in amber; the legend of the result has it as a fourth entry, "print supports", under added, unchanged and removed -- it has no card of its own), `.support_volume` and `.blockers`, and the overhangs it holds up are not listed as broken.
A block stops four steps of the field (about a tenth of a millimetre on a part of 100 mm) short of the edge of what it holds up, so that its own face is read cleanly; the check counts a place within those steps, and two more, of the block's edge as held, and finds the block under a face that leans at the depth of the clearance along the normal (the gap divided by the cosine of the lean), not straight `support_gap` down.
Block is the only kind so far; a rib or a column is a different shape of the same field, and a tree is a different problem (it has to grow round the part).

`export_print(result, "part.3mf")` writes the print as a 3MF with an **object for each**: the part (turned so that the way up of the print is z, as `.oriented()` does), the supports (type `support`) and each no-support
volume (type `other`, named `support_blocker_N`), all turned the same way. It is a file for a slicer to read: how a slicer treats the objects of type `support` and `other` is its own, and the file does not say
it in the slicer's own words (no slicer-specific modifier is written, because it could not be checked here). `export_mesh(result, ...)` writes the part alone, turned.

**Layers.** `export_layers(part, "layers.dxf", layer_height=0.2)` writes the contours of a body at a height every `layer_height` (through the middle of each layer): the outer contours and the holes as closed 3D
polylines, each layer on a DXF layer of its own (`L0001`, `L0002` ...), for a laser, a router or a program that stacks them. `export_slice(part, "slice.svg", z)` writes one layer as a picture, in millimetres. Both read the body's
field with the kernel's 2D contouring -- nothing is meshed -- and neither has paths, infill or perimeters: they are the shape of each layer, and not a toolpath.

## Speed

The result is a **field**, not a mesh: each remedy asks the part's own field for its value at other places (how far to the far side of a wall, what is above a point) and the viewport meshes that when it draws. The more the part's field costs, the more each of
those lookups costs, and an overhang gusset looks at about a hundred places. **The time of a result is the tool and the drawing added.** The walls, the gaps and the draft of a part whose field is a distance are formulas that ask the part's field dozens of times at every point the viewport meshes: exact, and a minute or more to draw. So the tool looks at what they add to the part **once**, on a lattice of the kernel (a quarter of a millimetre between nodes on a part of 90 mm), and **the window draws the part plus that lattice**: one lookup for each point, a second to draw where the formula takes a minute. The result itself -- the numbers, `manufacturing_analysis`, an export, `offset(result, 1)`, `difference` -- is the formula where a lattice is not exact, in thin zones round the kinks of the growth (the top of a drafted wall, a convex edge, the foot of a wall: a lattice reads a kink over one node), and the same lattice elsewhere, where the two agree to a thousandth of a millimetre; so a check or an export works the formula out only in those zones. The card says which one is drawn ("What is drawn is a lattice copy of the result"): the drawing has no zones, a lattice reads a kink over one node, which is finer than the cells the picture is made in, but you see it if you draw at a resolution finer than the nodes are apart. The remedies that are lattices of the kernel anyway -- the overhang, the corners, the undercut fill, and all the remedies of a part that is expensive to read, a topology-optimized bracket -- draw as they are made. Measured in the window of the program on a laptop processor, the first complete drawing of the four showcases of the developers' folder `tests/verification/manufacturing` takes 7.9 s for the printer's plate at 4 cells to the millimetre, 1.2 s for the mill's at 2, 0.9 s for the mould's at 2 and 5 s for the topology-optimized bracket at 1 (the scripts take 77 s, 32 s, 25 s and 5 minutes: they hold the independent checks). The drawing costs about the square of the resolution: **half as fine is about four times quicker**.
Draw at a coarser resolution first (`view.set_resolution(1.5)`), or run `manufacturing_analysis` on the result and `export_mesh` it -- an export meshes once, at the resolution you give it. The numbers (`.added`, `.removed`, `.fail_states`) do not depend on the picture.
To see where the drawing time goes, start the program with the environment variable `FIELDES_TIMING=1`: the log it writes ends with the `[dc]` lines of the mesher (the cells it worked through, the leaves on the surface and those with nothing in them, and the seconds each part took, summed over its threads).

## How it is tested

`examples/33_manufacturing_optimization.py` is the example (File ▸ Open example file...): a topology-optimized bracket -- struts of every thickness leaning every way, nothing designed for a process -- made ready for a printer, a mill and a mould, the plates and lugs that carry the load kept as they are, the printer's result drawn beside the bracket as it was and the mill's and the mould's models of the tree. The developers' own cases are in the repository's `tests/` folder: four showcases in `tests/verification/manufacturing/` -- a 3D printer's job (the check first, then a thin fin and a narrow slot made printable, a gusset grown under a ledge, a teardrop cut above a round hole), a mill's, a mould's, and the bracket again, each with the answers worked out by hand and the checks -- and about a hundred more cases, each trying to break one thing on a part made for it, in `tests/internal/` (`TEST_DESIGN.md` says why each exists, `JOB_TESTS.md` how the jobs are judged against published design rules, `proof/` holds the older proof scripts). No test exports a mesh.

## Scope

- **Supports are a block under what is left** (see above), after the geometry has been changed so that fewer are needed: a slicer's tree supports and its own tuning are where a slicer is better.
- **The files are for a slicer or a machine program to read**: the tool writes the 3MF, the layers and the slices, and no G-code.
- **A bridge longer than `max_bridge` and an overhang too wide to be filled down to a support are reported, with where they are**: the check and the card say so, and the shell does not change them (an arch or a gable is a change of design).
- **The numbers are the part's own field, checked by a second method, not a print**: run your own check, and print the part.
